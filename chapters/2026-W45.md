# The Unraveling Hum

The phone line hummed, not with a dial tone, but a low, guttural growl that bled through the receiver. It wasn't the usual Blackwood Bay interference; this felt heavier, thicker, as if the static wasn't just noise but a viscous, hungry presence trying to consume the words before they could reach my ear. I tried Miller’s number again, the town clerk who’d suddenly gone quiet on me. Nothing but that pervasive buzz, a kind of digital maw.

Outside, the perpetual gray of Blackwood Bay pressed against the windowpane, blurring the skeletal branches of the pines. A faint tremor shivered through the floorboards, a barely perceptible groan from the earth itself. The old ceramic mug on my desk, half-filled with cold coffee, rattled a soft, anxious rhythm against the scarred oak. It wasn't enough to spill, never was, but it was enough to remind you that the ground beneath your feet was never quite still in this town. It hummed with a restless energy that echoed the static in my ear, a feeling of something constantly being adjusted, or worse, *taken*.

I hung up, the silence that followed the static feeling almost deafening. Miller’s abrupt radio silence wasn't just a missed call; it was another brick missing from the foundation of Blackwood Bay, another piece of the puzzle that refused to lock into place. The tremors, the static, the sudden disappearance of reliable sources – they weren't isolated incidents. They were the atmosphere itself, tightening its grip, turning the air into a question mark.

But the silence that followed the static didn't last. Instead, there was *this*. This low thrum, a dull ache behind my teeth that had started sometime after midnight, vibrating not just through the old floorboards of my office, but in the marrow of my bones. A pervasive, almost electrical current in the air, making the condensation on my forgotten coffee cup shiver minutely. Outside, the familiar, mournful bellow of the old foghorn, a sound that had been the town’s bedrock for generations, had gone silent. Utterly, unnervingly silent. No one remembered that happening before, not once in living memory. The quiet felt louder than any storm.

I tried to force my thoughts back to the crime scene photos spread across my cluttered desk: the unnaturally precise scorch marks on the Peterson cabin floor, the way the shadows in the periphery seemed to deepen with each print. I traced a finger over a grainy image of a child's toy, half-buried in ash, untouched by the blaze. Every concrete detail, every shard of evidence, felt weighted down by the strange atmosphere. The hum pushed at the edges of my concentration, a slow, sustained pressure building somewhere unseen, like a deep-sea engine winding up for a voyage no one asked for.

I picked up a faded polaroid of the missing girl, Eleanor Vance, her smile too bright against the deepening gloom of the Blackwood woods. The photo seemed to hum in my hand, echoing the town's unsettling pulse. I felt the cold certainty that the silence of the foghorn and the low, vibrational hum were not just distractions, but pieces of the same unnerving puzzle, signals from a world that was slowly, deliberately, pulling itself apart at the seams.
