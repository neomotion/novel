# The Rooted Hum

David tightened his grip on the pen. He had a feeling this wasn’t just a story about pollution. The geometric structure, the ‘song,’ the precise coordinates – it wasn't a distant anomaly anymore. It was an active, growing influence, its tendrils reaching into Blackwood Bay itself, transforming the very air, the ground, the static on the radio. The town wasn't just mirroring the chaos; it was becoming an extension of it. He looked out at the iridescent film on the water, then at the distant, watchful faces of the old timers. The silence now was a promise, but of what, he couldn’t yet say. He felt the familiar thrum beneath his feet, a low, persistent pulse, and knew, with a chilling certainty, that whatever had risen from the depths was now very much awake, and its presence was beginning to echo in the very fabric of Blackwood Bay, demanding a response. He had to find a way back out there, deeper this time, before the town itself became another vanishing point on his map.His office was an anchor against the rising tide of strangeness, a place of paper and ink where facts, however mutable, still held some weight. The next morning, the halogen bulb above David Vance’s desk shuddered, casting a strobe of flickering light across the faded blueprint pinned to the corkboard. It was the original diagram for the old Blackwood Point lighthouse, a structure long since declared unsafe. A persistent, almost musical hum had taken residence in the building’s wiring all day, a fragile thing that now threatened to snap, plunging the office into the kind of deep gloom Blackwood Bay specialized in.Just as the hum seemed to waver on the brink of silence, a low, deep vibration began to pulse up through the floorboards. It wasn't violent, just a pervasive tremor, as if the entire town was exhaling, or perhaps bracing. A half-empty mug of cold coffee shimmied an inch across the blotter. Vance’s eyes, however, stayed fixed on the blueprint. He traced a finger along the spiral staircase detail, noticing for the first time a series of hairline fractures marked in red pencil, almost invisible under the shifting light. The cracks weren’t random; they followed the contours of an old, perhaps forgotten, service tunnel that led from the base of the lighthouse, angling inland. The town, like the paper beneath his hand, felt brittle, about to give.The air in Blackwood Bay had been holding its breath for days, or perhaps it was holding mine. A low, persistent thrum vibrated through the floorboards of his office, a bass note beneath a profound, unnatural quiet. Not the peace of an empty forest, but a silence that felt deliberately enforced, as if some cosmic hand had muted the world beyond his window. Even the old police band radio on his desk, usually a babbling stream of local chatter and static, offered only a whispery, intermittent crackle, like dry leaves scuttling across a tombstone. It was a broken sound, a fractured signal from a world gone strangely inert.He traced a finger over the worn lines of the Blackwood Bay map tacked to his corkboard, a web of streets and forgotten paths leading to the water's edge. The thrum seemed to rise from the very granite the town clung to, a tremor that settled deep in the bones, not violent, but pervasive. It felt less like the earth shifting and more like the town itself sighing a slow, painful breath. His notes on Elara Thorne, scrawled alongside obscure historical markers, seemed to pulse faintly in the dim light, illuminated by the sheer weight of the atmosphere. He saw her name again, a ghost on the page, intertwined with faded notations about old logging routes and rumored sub-surface channels beneath the town.The unnerving quiet pressed in, heavier now, punctuated by the almost subliminal vibration. It made the mundane details of his investigation — the missing files, the hushed rumors of the lighthouse keeper, the strange bloom of a certain fungi near Miller's Pond — feel suddenly sharper, more urgent. The fungi, a sickly violet, had begun appearing overnight, clinging to damp stone and rotting wood, its tendrils reaching out with an unnatural speed. Blackwood Bay wasn't just quiet; it was listening. And whatever it was listening for, it felt like it was about to find it.The generator hummed a strained, off-key chord beneath David Vance's feet. It was the third time the grid had hiccuped that hour, plunging his office into a momentary, deeper gloom before the emergency lights flickered on, painting the walls in a sickly yellow. He didn't bother looking up from the faded map of Blackwood Bay spread across his desk. The town was restless, its electrical arteries struggling, and it bled into the air, a hum that felt less like machinery and more like a fever dream.His old police scanner, usually a reliable source of local static and mundane dispatches, was worse. It wasn't just interference; it was a rhythmic, almost deliberate pulse buried beneath the white noise, a sound like gravel turning in a loose machine. He'd been trying to decode the fragmented transmissions from the abandoned lighthouse, rumors of a strange light seen just before the disappearances began. But tonight, the airwaves felt choked, as if something invisible had pressed a palm against the signal, distorting it, making it listen. The static seemed to resolve into the metallic tang he’d tasted on the pier, the industrial scent now a palpable presence in the airwaves.Vance leaned back, the springs of his chair groaning a complaint. The city lights outside his window, usually a fractured patchwork, now seemed to wink in an unsteady rhythm, a collective eye struggling to stay open. He took a sip of cold coffee, its bitterness a familiar anchor. The Bay was whispering, and its secrets weren't just hidden; they were being actively garbled, swallowed by a deeper, more pervasive noise.David Vance sat hunched over his notes, the cheap battery-powered recorder on the table beside him sputtering. It wasn't the battery; lately, everything digital in Blackwood Bay seemed afflicted with a creeping lethargy, a hesitation. The signal on his portable radio, usually crisp even for the late-night local reports, was a constant wash of low-frequency static, a murmur that felt less like interference and more like a sigh. He tapped the recorder, but the playback remained garbled, voices stretched thin as if through water, like the echo of a forgotten conversation.He glanced up, catching his own reflection in the darkened window of his office. For a fleeting second, the glass seemed to hold not just his image, but a vague, deeper ripple behind him, a distortion that suggested another presence observing from the gloom of the room. He turned, the floorboards creaking, but found only the familiar stacks of case files and the cold, unmoving shadow of his desk lamp. The air felt heavy, charged with an invisible weight, and the objects in the room – the worn armchair, the overflowing ashtray – seemed to possess a strange, expectant stillness, as if holding their breath.Beneath his worn boots, a faint vibration thrummed through the floorboards. It wasn't the traffic outside; Blackwood Bay slept deep this late. It was a low, resonant hum that seemed to originate from beneath the very bedrock of the town, a guttural purr that made the water in his forgotten coffee mug shiver almost imperceptibly. He leaned closer to the map tacked to his wall, tracing the rumored fault lines of the old mining tunnels. The hum intensified, a silent testament to something stirring deep below, mimicking the static’s insidious crawl across his equipment – a slow, deliberate interference with the solid world.The cheap plastic cup of lukewarm coffee vibrated on David Vance’s desk, a low, persistent hum resonating not from the street outside, but from somewhere deeper, under the old floorboards of his Blackwood Bay office. It wasn’t a passing truck, nor the town's ancient generator. It was something else, a pulse in the bedrock, a slow, unsettling rhythm that had started days ago and hadn’t let up, rattling the dust on forgotten files.He ran a hand over the grainy photo of the abandoned lighthouse keeper’s cottage, rumored to have been silent for decades. The silence in the bay itself had grown strange, not just quiet, but *empty*. The usual squabble of gulls, the distant cry of children from the park — all muted, swallowed. Only the static on the old police scanner in the corner offered a reprieve, a constant hiss that sometimes, for a split second, resolved into a faint, distorted snippet of a child’s counting rhyme, something about 'one, two, three little shadows.' It was too clear for static, too specific for a hallucination.David leaned back, the springs of his chair protesting the shift. Blackwood Bay always hummed with its own secrets, but lately, the hum was literal, a deep thrumming that seemed to emanate from the very earth. The secrets felt heavier, pressing down like the thick, salt-laced fog that often rolled in off the coast. He traced the lines of an old nautical chart, a forgotten channel marked 'Devil's Throat,' and wondered if the town wasn't just hiding things, but *feeling* them, vibrating with an unspoken dread that echoed his own. He knew what he had to do. He had to go back to the lighthouse.He pushed through the overgrown path leading to the old lighthouse, the air thick and sweet with the smell of damp earth and something else, something cloyingly green. The ferns here, always robust, seemed to have taken on an impossible vibrancy, their fronds unfurling like alien hands. Even the birds were quiet, a silence that felt heavier than sound. It wasn't natural, this sudden, aggressive bloom in late autumn. It felt like the forest was holding its breath, or exhaling something new and unsettling.He stopped at the edge of the bluff, where the wind usually whipped the sea spray into a frenzy. Today, it was just a whisper, a low moan that seemed to rise from the very stones beneath his feet. A hairline crack, fresh and stark, snaked its way across the concrete base of the lighthouse, following no clear pattern. It hadn't been there yesterday. He pressed a gloved hand against the cold stone, feeling a faint, almost imperceptible vibration, like a distant pulse deep underground. Blackwood Bay had its secrets, but lately, they felt less like secrets and more like a fever spreading through the land itself. He knelt, tracing the new crack with his finger, watching it spiderweb near the faint scorch marks he’d noted earlier. A low hum resonated from the stone itself, a familiar thrum that mirrored the one from his office. As he stood, he felt a sudden, dizzying sense of disorientation, a brief warping of perception, as if the ground itself had shifted beneath him. The air grew colder, and a sharp, metallic tang, like the industrial smell from the bay, filled his nostrils. He glanced up at the lighthouse, its old glass eye staring blankly at the sea, and then down at the new, almost glowing, sickly violet fungi creeping across the foundation, towards the tunnel entrance. He had found his way back, but what he found here felt less like an answer and more like an opening. The hum grew, and he realized it wasn't just in the ground, but in his head, a song he was beginning to recognize.
