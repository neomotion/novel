# The Bedrock Pulse

The halogen bulb above David Vance’s desk shuddered, casting a strobe of light across the faded blueprint pinned to the corkboard. It was the original diagram for the old Blackwood Point lighthouse, a structure long since declared unsafe, now given a spectral life by the erratic light. A persistent, almost musical hum had taken residence in the building’s wiring all day, a fragile thing that now threatened to snap, plunging the office into the kind of deep gloom Blackwood Bay specialized in. He had returned from the pier with the chilling memory of the iridescent slick, that perfect, industrial sheen, and the inexplicable 'click' still resonating in his ears.

Just as the hum seemed to waver on the brink of silence, a low, deep vibration began to pulse up through the floorboards. It wasn't violent, just a pervasive tremor, as if the entire town was exhaling, or perhaps bracing. A half-empty mug of cold coffee shimmied an inch across the blotter, tracing an arc on the worn wood. Vance’s eyes, however, stayed fixed on the blueprint. He traced a finger along the spiral staircase detail, noticing for the first time a series of hairline fractures marked in red pencil, almost invisible under the shifting light. The town, like the paper beneath his hand, felt brittle, about to give. The gate was not merely opening; it was grinding through the very foundations of Blackwood Bay.

The air in Blackwood Bay had been holding its breath for days, or perhaps it was holding his. A low, persistent thrum vibrated through the floorboards of his office, a bass note beneath a profound, unnatural quiet. Not the peace of an empty forest, but a silence that felt deliberately enforced, as if some cosmic hand had muted the world beyond his window. Even the old police band radio on his desk, usually a babbling stream of local chatter and static, offered only a whispery, intermittent crackle, like dry leaves scuttling across a tombstone. It was a broken sound, a fractured signal from a world gone strangely inert. The metallic tang in the air had deepened, a coppery taste that clung to the back of his throat.

He traced a finger over the worn lines of the Blackwood Bay map tacked to his corkboard, a web of streets and forgotten paths leading to the water's edge. The thrum seemed to rise from the very granite the town clung to, a tremor that settled deep in the bones, not violent, but pervasive. It felt less like the earth shifting and more like the town itself sighing a slow, painful breath. His notes on Eleanor Vance, scrawled alongside obscure historical markers he’d been researching, seemed to pulse faintly in the dim light, illuminated by the sheer weight of the atmosphere. The name, so close to his own, felt like a deliberate pull, a thread from a tapestry he hadn't known he was a part of.

The unnerving quiet pressed in, heavier now, punctuated by the almost subliminal vibration. It made the mundane details of his investigation — the missing files, the hushed rumors of the lighthouse keeper, the strange bloom of a certain fungi near Miller's Pond — feel suddenly sharper, more urgent. Blackwood Bay wasn't just quiet; it was listening. And whatever it was listening for, it felt like it was about to find it. The generator hummed a strained, off-key chord beneath his feet moments later. It was the third time the grid had hiccuped that hour, plunging his office into a momentary, deeper gloom before the emergency lights flickered on, painting the walls in a sickly yellow. He didn't bother looking up from the faded map of Blackwood Bay spread across his desk. The town was restless, its electrical arteries struggling, and it bled into the air, a hum that felt less like machinery and more like a fever dream.

His old police scanner, usually a reliable source of local static and mundane dispatches, was worse. It wasn't just interference; it was a rhythmic, almost deliberate pulse buried beneath the white noise, a sound like gravel turning in a loose machine. He'd been trying to decode the fragmented transmissions from the abandoned lighthouse, rumors of a strange light seen just before the disappearances began. But tonight, the airwaves felt choked, as if something invisible had pressed a palm against the signal, distorting it, making it listen. The flickering streetlights outside winked in an unsteady rhythm, a collective eye struggling to stay open.

Vance leaned back, the springs of his chair groaning a complaint. The Bay was whispering, and its secrets weren't just hidden; they were being actively garbled, swallowed by a deeper, more pervasive noise. David sat hunched over his notes, the cheap battery-powered recorder on the table beside him sputtering. It wasn't the battery; lately, everything digital in Blackwood Bay seemed afflicted with a creeping lethargy, a hesitation. The signal on his portable radio, usually crisp even for the late-night local reports, was a constant wash of low-frequency static, a murmur that felt less like interference and more like a sigh. He tapped the recorder, but the playback remained garbled, voices stretched thin as if through water.

He glanced up, catching his own reflection in the darkened window of his office. For a fleeting second, the glass seemed to hold not just his image, but a vague, deeper ripple behind him, a distortion that suggested another presence observing from the gloom of the room. He turned, the floorboards creaking, but found only the familiar stacks of case files and the cold, unmoving shadow of his desk lamp. The air felt heavy, charged with an invisible weight, and the objects in the room – the worn armchair, the overflowing ashtray – seemed to possess a strange, expectant stillness, as if holding their breath.

Beneath his worn boots, a faint vibration thrummed through the floorboards. It wasn't the traffic outside; Blackwood Bay slept deep this late. It was a low, resonant hum that seemed to originate from beneath the very bedrock of the town, a guttural purr that made the water in his forgotten coffee mug shiver almost imperceptibly. He leaned closer to the map tacked to his wall, tracing the rumored fault lines of the old mining tunnels. The hum intensified, a silent testament to something stirring deep below, mimicking the static’s insidious crawl across his equipment – a slow, deliberate interference with the solid world.

The cheap plastic cup of lukewarm coffee vibrated on David Vance’s desk, a low, persistent hum resonating not from the street outside, but from somewhere deeper, under the old floorboards of his Blackwood Bay office. It wasn’t a passing truck, nor the town's ancient generator. It was something else, a pulse in the bedrock, a slow, unsettling rhythm that had started two days ago and hadn’t let up, rattling the dust on forgotten files. The memory of the iridescent slick on the bay felt like a prelude to this deeper, more pervasive internal tremor.

He ran a hand over the grainy photo of the abandoned lighthouse keeper’s cottage, rumored to have been silent for decades. The silence in the bay itself had grown strange, not just quiet, but *empty*. The usual squabble of gulls, the distant cry of children from the park — all muted, swallowed. Only the static on the old police scanner in the corner offered a reprieve, a constant hiss that sometimes, for a split second, resolved into a faint, distorted snippet of a child’s counting rhyme, something about 'one, two, three little shadows.'

David leaned back, the springs of his chair protesting the shift. Blackwood Bay always hummed with its own secrets, but lately, the hum was literal, a deep thrumming that seemed to emanate from the very earth. The secrets felt heavier, pressing down like the thick, salt-laced fog that often rolled in off the coast. He traced the lines of an old nautical chart, a forgotten channel marked 'Devil's Throat,' and wondered if the town wasn't just hiding things, but *feeling* them, vibrating with an unspoken dread that echoed his own. He knew what he had to do. The lighthouse, the source of fragmented transmissions, the symbol of the town's failing structure, was calling to him directly.

David pushed through the overgrown path leading to the old lighthouse, the air thick and sweet with the smell of damp earth and something else, something cloyingly green. The ferns here, always robust, seemed to have taken on an impossible vibrancy, their fronds unfurling like alien hands. Even the birds were quiet, a silence that felt heavier than sound. It wasn't natural, this sudden, aggressive bloom in late autumn. It felt like the forest was holding its breath, or exhaling something new and unsettling. The hum here was different, more visceral, a low groan rising directly from the ground.

He stopped at the edge of the bluff, where the wind usually whipped the sea spray into a frenzy. Today, it was just a whisper, a low moan that seemed to rise from the very stones beneath his feet. A hairline crack, fresh and stark, snaked its way across the concrete base of the lighthouse, following no clear pattern. It hadn't been there yesterday. He pressed a gloved hand against the cold stone, feeling a faint, almost imperceptible vibration, like a distant pulse deep underground. Blackwood Bay had its secrets, but lately, they felt less like secrets and more like a fever spreading through the land itself, infecting its very bones and flora. He pulled his hand back, a cold certainty solidifying in his gut. The cracks weren't just structural damage; they were a wound. And inside the lighthouse, from its darkened lantern room, he could just make out a faint, rhythmic pulsing light, a beacon no longer guiding ships, but signalling something else entirely. Something was about to break through.
