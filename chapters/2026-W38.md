# The Deep Hum

The old sedan coughed its way down Saltmarsh Road, headlights carving weak tunnels through the thick, salt-laced air. David Vance had the radio on, a habit born of long nights chasing stories across the empty stretches of Blackwood Bay. Tonight, its usual cacophony of local ads and half-hearted news segments was replaced by a low, persistent hiss. It wasn’t just static; there was a pattern to it now, an almost rhythmic pulse beneath the white noise, like a distant heart beating a fractured code. He’d tried other stations, but they were all the same, tuned to this phantom frequency, or worse, utterly silent. The bay itself was holding its breath. The usual clamor of gulls, the mournful blare of the lighthouse foghorn, even the distant thrum of fishing trawlers—all were absent, swallowed by a stillness that felt less like peace and more like a void, a held note before a storm.

He pulled up to the abandoned dockmaster’s shack, its windows like vacant eyes staring out at the oily black water. He was here because the last report of the *Sea Serpent*—one of the three trawlers that had simply… ceased—had placed it near this derelict stretch, an anomaly in a town built on maritime routine. The silence outside was profound, broken only by the rhythmic whisper of the radio, which now seemed to be trying to form something, a sound on the edge of comprehension. A shiver, not from the cold, traced its way up his spine. Reaching for the ignition, he paused, hand hovering over the key. He could feel it then, a deep, resonant hum vibrating through the floorboards of the car, through the very soles of his shoes. It wasn’t the engine, nor the wind. It was coming from somewhere beneath, from the wet earth, from the unseen depths of the bay. The glass of the passenger window rattled faintly, a sympathetic tremor to a much larger, unspoken disturbance.

He cut the engine, plunging the small space into absolute quiet save for the radio’s alien pulse. The hum intensified, a low, guttural vibration that promised to rearrange the very foundations of the world he knew. Blackwood Bay was always a place of strange undercurrents, but tonight, they felt closer to the surface, thrumming with a unified, unsettling intention. He grabbed his notepad and a flashlight, the concrete details of his investigation feeling flimsy against the growing, unseen weight in the air. The missing trawlers, the impossible low tides that had begun to expose things better left buried—they were all converging on this single, disquieting note.

The next day, David Vance stood on the old Blackwood Bay pier, the rotten wood groaning under his weight more than the tide. He had spent the morning interviewing Agnes at the diner, piecing together the fragmented reports of what the receding waters had revealed: strange, geometric scorch marks on bedrock, patterns of phosphorescence that faded with the dawn, and the unsettling silence that had fallen over the fishing community. The salt wind bit, carrying with it a faint, almost subliminal hum that wasn’t the ocean, wasn’t the static on his old shortwave radio. It was deeper, colder, a current that seemed to flow directly into the bone, whispering a clarity that felt less like truth and more like a carefully constructed illusion. He checked the tide charts in his waterproof notebook, the ink bleeding slightly at the corners, trying to find a pattern in the recent, anomalous low tides that had exposed these strange things along the shoreline, searching for a correlation with the hum.

The air itself felt thick, not with fog, but with an unseen pressure. Beneath his worn boots, the pier seemed to shiver, a continuous, barely perceptible ripple that wasn’t the waves. It was the land itself, shifting, resettling its ancient bones, a low, guttural thrum vibrating up through the pilings, through the concrete of the bay walls. Not an earthquake, not yet, but the promise of one, a slow exhale from beneath the town that made the distant gulls sound like panicked sirens, their cries distorted by the growing hum.

He pulled his collar tighter against the chill, the hum in his marrow intensifying, a siren song promising answers if only he’d surrender to its cold logic. Vance didn't trust easy answers, especially not the ones that resonated without a source. He stared out at the dark, roiling water, knowing the answers he sought were concrete, solid, yet the world around him was melting into something else, a dream he was forced to navigate with a reporter's notebook and a flashlight against a coming, synthetic dawn. The coordinates given by the harbor master for the *Sea Serpent's* last ping danced in his peripheral vision, mocking his search for logic in a town that had forgotten it.

The office felt colder than the November night pressing against the windows. David Vance stared at the blinking cursor on his screen, the last light in a row of darkened storefronts along Main Street. Blackwood Bay always had eyes, he knew, but lately, they felt less like the usual small-town scrutiny and more like something poised, waiting. A low thrum, deep in the foundations of the old building, vibrated faintly through his desk, a sound he'd come to associate with the town's restless core. He wasn't just digging for stories anymore; he was digging under a palpable weight, a suffocating scrutiny that hadn't been there a month ago, making him feel less like a journalist and more like a target.

He flicked on the battered transistor radio beside his coffee cup, hoping for the mundane drone of late-night talk or a forgotten blues track. Instead, the airwaves crackled with something else. Not just static, but a deliberate chorus of fractured tones, like distant, multi-layered whispers trying to coalesce, failing, then trying again. It wasn't human, not exactly, but it held a peculiar, almost intelligent rhythm, a language of interference that pulled at the edges of his concentration. He leaned closer, trying to discern a word, a pattern, anything beyond the unnerving sonic tapestry. He’d cross-referenced the coordinates against seismic data, tidal anomalies, anything that might explain the hum and the missing boats, but there was nothing ordinary, only this deepening silence and the rising note beneath it.

The radio coughed, momentarily clearing to a local weather forecast—a prediction of more unseasonable calm—then plunged back into the cacophony. He turned the dial slowly, past every station, but the peculiar signal seemed to follow, a persistent phantom in the ether. It hummed in time with the deeper tremor from below, a duet of the unnerving, reminding him that Blackwood Bay's secrets weren't just buried in court documents or hushed conversations. They were in the very air, in the earth, and now, it seemed, in the signals that rode the waves, all of it watching him, waiting for him to make the wrong move.

The coffee in his chipped mug trembled the next morning, not enough to spill, but enough to catch the light in a perpetual ripple. It wasn’t the old diner’s usual rumble; this was deeper, a low-frequency hum that felt less like sound and more like a pressure in the bones. It had been like this for days, a constant, almost imperceptible tremor running beneath Blackwood Bay, making the floorboards sigh and the streetlights hum a pitch too low. Agnes, the woman behind the counter, wiped it down with a slow, mechanical precision, her eyes, usually sharp, now distant. “Everyone’s just… tired, David,” she’d murmured yesterday, “Like the energy’s just been sucked right out of us.”

He’d heard similar whispers down by the abandoned cannery, where the air itself seemed to vibrate with a peculiar emptiness. It wasn’t just simple fatigue; it was a quiet, insidious drainage, leaving echoes where substance should be. Even the sound of the ocean, usually a constant roar, seemed muted, hollowed out, as if the very noise of the world was being siphoned away, replaced by that pervasive, low drone.

His notes on the missing fishermen lay spread across the worn Formica, each line feeling heavier under the pervasive thrum. The *Sea Serpent*, the *Lucky Charm*, the *Ocean's Promise*—three boats, nine men, gone without a trace, without even a storm to blame. The last known coordinates were still etched onto the tide charts he’d procured from the harbour master—a set of numbers that pulsed faintly in his peripheral vision, a strange, precise repetition, a mirror of the Bay’s unsettling rhythm. They hadn't vanished in a storm; they simply ceased, leaving behind only the ghost of their departure, a silence too perfect. The coordinates wouldn't let him go: precisely 7.76 nautical miles from the buoy marking the outer edge of Blackwood Shoals, a detail too specific to be random, too quiet to ignore. The pattern on the radio, the hum, the missing boats, the strange tides—they all converged on that single, impossible point in the dark water. He decided then, he had to go.
