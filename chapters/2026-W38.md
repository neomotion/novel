# The Thrumming Bay

The old sedan coughed its way down Saltmarsh Road, a sound David Vance had come to associate with his own reluctance. Headlights carved weak tunnels through the thick, salt-laced air, pushing back the encroaching black. He hadn't wanted to come back to Blackwood Bay, not after twenty years, not after Iris. But the missing boats, the whispers, the sheer, impossible silence that had fallen over the town like a shroud – they’d dragged him in, a hook in a gut wound.

He’d barely killed the engine at the abandoned dockmaster’s shack before he saw it. Tucked under the windshield wiper, crisp and stark white against the grime, was a single sheet of paper. Typed. In his own voice. He didn’t remember writing it. His breath hitched as he read:

*The old sedan coughed its way down Saltmarsh Road, headlights carving weak tunnels through the thick, salt-laced air. David Vance had the radio on, a habit more than a comfort, its usual cacophony of local ads and half-hearted news segments replaced by a low, persistent hiss. It wasn't just static; there was a pattern to it now, an almost rhythmic pulse beneath the white noise, like a distant heart beating a fractured code. He’d tried other stations, but they were all the same, tuned to this phantom frequency, or worse, utterly silent. The bay itself was holding its breath tonight. The usual clamor of gulls, the mournful blare of the lighthouse foghorn, even the distant thrum of fishing trawlers – all were absent, swallowed by a stillness that felt less like peace and more like a void.*

*He pulled up to the abandoned dockmaster’s shack, its windows like vacant eyes staring out at the oily black water. The silence outside was profound, broken only by the rhythmic whisper of the radio, which now seemed to be trying to form something. A shiver, not from the cold, traced its way up his spine. Reaching for the ignition, he paused, hand hovering over the key. He could feel it then, a deep, resonant hum vibrating through the floorboards of the car, through the very soles of his shoes. It wasn't the engine, nor the wind. It was coming from somewhere beneath, from the wet earth, from the unseen depths of the bay. The glass of the passenger window rattled faintly, a sympathetic tremor to a much larger, unspoken disturbance.*

*He cut the engine, plunging the small space into absolute quiet save for the radio’s alien pulse. The hum intensified, a low, guttural vibration that promised to rearrange the very foundations of the world he knew. Blackwood Bay was always a place of strange undercurrents, but tonight, they felt closer to the surface, thrumming with a unified, unsettling intention. He grabbed his notepad and a flashlight, the concrete details of his investigation feeling flimsy against the growing, unseen weight in the air.*

David reread the page, his mind a scramble of disbelief and cold dread. This wasn't a story he’d written. This was... now. Had he typed it in some fugue state? He had his old Remington in the trunk, a relic he swore he hadn't used in years. But who else? He grabbed his notepad and a flashlight, the concrete details of his investigation feeling flimsy against the growing, unseen weight in the air. The hum, as described on the page, was undeniably real, vibrating through the soles of his shoes, a low, guttural thrum from the earth itself.

The next morning, the unease hadn't faded. He found himself on the old Blackwood Bay pier, the rotten wood groaning under his weight more than the tide. Another page, this one folded neatly and wedged into a crack in the pilings, had his name on it. Or rather, *his voice* on it. It told him what he was seeing, what he was feeling, even the hum that now seemed to flow directly into the bone, whispering a clarity that felt less like truth and more like a carefully constructed illusion. He checked the tide charts in his waterproof notebook, the ink bleeding slightly at the corners, trying to find a pattern in the recent, anomalous low tides that had exposed strange things along the shoreline – things he couldn't quite identify, barnacled shapes that defied simple biology. The air itself felt thick, not with fog, but with an unseen pressure.

Beneath his worn boots, the pier seemed to shiver, a continuous, barely perceptible ripple that wasn't the waves. It was the land itself, shifting, resettling its ancient bones, a low, guttural thrum vibrating up through the pilings, through the concrete of the bay walls. Not an earthquake, not yet, but the promise of one, a slow exhale from beneath the town that made the distant gulls sound like panicked sirens, their cries distorted by the growing hum. He pulled his collar tighter against the chill, the hum in his marrow intensifying, a siren song promising answers if only he'd surrender to its cold logic. Vance didn't trust easy answers, especially not the ones that resonated without a source. He stared out at the dark, roiling water, knowing the answers he sought were concrete, solid, yet the world around him was melting into something else, a dream he was forced to navigate with a reporter's notebook and a flashlight against a coming, synthetic dawn.

The office felt colder than the November night pressing against the windows, despite the fact that it was only early September. David Vance stared at the blinking cursor on his screen, the last light in a row of darkened storefronts along Main Street. Another page, this one slipped under his office door while he was out, had chronicled his drive back, the hum in the car, the unsettling quiet of the street. Blackwood Bay always had eyes, he knew, but lately, they felt less like the usual small-town scrutiny and more like something poised, waiting. A low thrum, deep in the foundations of the old building, vibrated faintly through his desk, a sound he'd come to associate with the town's restless core. He wasn't just digging for stories anymore; he was digging under a palpable weight, a suffocating scrutiny that hadn't been there a month ago, making him feel less like a journalist and more like a target.

He flicked on the battered transistor radio beside his coffee cup, hoping for the mundane drone of late-night talk or a forgotten blues track. Instead, the airwaves crackled with something else. Not just static, but a deliberate chorus of fractured tones, like distant, multi-layered whispers trying to coalesce, failing, then trying again. It wasn't human, not exactly, but it held a peculiar, almost intelligent rhythm, a language of interference that pulled at the edges of his concentration. He leaned closer, trying to discern a word, a pattern, anything beyond the unnerving sonic tapestry. The radio coughed, momentarily clearing to a local weather forecast—a dire warning of an approaching cold front, unusually early in the season—then plunged back into the cacophony. He turned the dial slowly, past every station, but the peculiar signal seemed to follow, a persistent phantom in the ether. It hummed in time with the deeper tremor from below, a duet of the unnerving, reminding him that Blackwood Bay's secrets weren't just buried in court documents or hushed conversations. They were in the very air, in the earth, and now, it seemed, in the signals that rode the waves, all of it watching him, waiting for him to make the wrong move.

The coffee in his chipped mug trembled, not enough to spill, but enough to catch the light in a perpetual ripple. It wasn't the old diner's usual rumble; this was deeper, a low-frequency hum that felt less like sound and more like a pressure in the bones. It had been like this for days, a constant, almost imperceptible tremor running beneath Blackwood Bay, making the floorboards sigh and the streetlights hum a pitch too low. Agnes, the woman behind the counter, wiped it down with a slow, mechanical precision, her eyes, usually sharp, now distant. 'Everyone's just… tired, David,' she'd murmured yesterday, 'Like the energy's just been sucked right out of us.' Her movements were slower now, her voice a little flatter. David recognized the hollow echo in her words, the subtle, insidious drainage that left behind only a murmuring shade. This was how it started.

He’d heard similar whispers down by the abandoned cannery, where the air itself seemed to vibrate with a peculiar emptiness. It wasn't just simple fatigue; it was a quiet, insidious drainage, leaving echoes where substance should be. Even the sound of the ocean, usually a constant roar, seemed muted, hollowed out, as if the very noise of the world was being siphoned away, replaced by that pervasive, low drone.

His notes on the missing fishermen lay spread across the worn Formica, each line feeling heavier under the pervasive thrum. He traced the last known coordinates etched onto the tide charts he’d procured from the harbour master – a set of numbers that pulsed faintly in his peripheral vision, a strange, precise repetition, a mirror of the Bay’s unsettling rhythm. They hadn't vanished in a storm; they simply ceased, leaving behind only the ghost of their departure, a silence too perfect. The numbers wouldn't let him go, precisely 7.76 nautical miles from the buoy, a detail too specific to be random, too quiet to ignore. His flashlight, a heavy-duty beam, felt like a meager defense against the growing darkness that seemed to be actively *absorbing* the light from the diner. Even the neon sign outside seemed to dim as he watched.

The coordinates led to a stretch of water notoriously calm, historically unproductive, yet inexplicably deep. David decided to go. He needed facts, something to anchor to, even if the facts were written on pages he didn't remember producing. He waited until nightfall, the town's lights flickering, the lighthouse beam noticeably weaker tonight, a hesitant pulse against the encroaching void. The moment he stepped from his car at the deserted shore, the darkness thickened, pressing in, not just the absence of light, but a palpable, viscous entity. The hum, no longer distant, pulsed around him, *through* him, a low, guttural vibration that seemed to emanate from the very air.

His flashlight beam cut a weak swath through the gloom. Murmurs began, soft and fragmented, like windblown whispers. He saw shapes at the edge of his light, barely-there figures that shifted, coalesced, then melted back into the deeper dark. Not entirely human, but echoing human forms, their voices repeating snatches of conversations, fragments of lost lives. *"The net... just the one..." "Seven... she was only seven..."* The words were like a cold claw down his spine. Iris. He pressed forward, drawn by the numbers, a foolish, journalistic courage overriding his fear.

Then the hum intensified, shifting from a pervasive thrum to a focused, malicious roar. The shadowy forms solidified, their movements quicker, less phantom. They weren't just echoes; they were *hungry*. He stumbled, the sand pulling at his shoes, the beam of his flashlight flickering precariously. The living dark surged, a wave of cold, silent pressure that pushed against him, reaching, grasping. He heard his own ragged breath, the desperate thump of his heart. The lighthouse beam, a distant, dying star, was his only hope. He broke into a run, the shadows closing in behind him, their murmurs growing into a chilling chorus, a dark tide rising from the bay, demanding a story. He barely made it to his car, fumbling with the keys, throwing himself inside, locking the doors just as a shadowy hand slapped against the window, leaving a faint, oily smear. He drove, not knowing where, only knowing he had to find light, any light.

Back at his office, shaken and sweating, another page lay on his desk, already there, waiting. It described his desperate flight, the pursuing dark, the faint echo of Iris's age in the murmurs. *“The living dark had tasted him, and it wanted more. It wanted the story finished, and it had chosen him to write it.”* David stared at the words, the typewriter in the corner of the room, dust-covered and silent, accusing him. Was he merely documenting Blackwood Bay’s final chapter, or was he, unknowingly, writing its end? The question hung heavy in the thrumming air, a cold, undeniable truth. And he knew, with chilling certainty, that the story was only just beginning.
