# The Submerged Cadence

The bay was waiting. David Vance felt the low thrum resonate in his chest as he navigated the narrow gangway to his rented skiff, the *Sea Ghost*. The air, thick with the brine of the sea and something else – a faint, metallic tang – vibrated with a low, persistent hum. It wasn't the wind, nor the distant drone of a trawler. It felt deeper, resonating in his sternum like a misplaced heartbeat, an echo of some internal tremor the earth itself couldn't quite contain.

He climbed into the small, cramped cabin, the old police scanner on the console sputtering to life with a jolt. It wasn't the usual white noise; fragments of sound, impossibly deep and devoid of human inflection, ripped through the air – not words, exactly, but data corrupted, like a digital scream from somewhere beyond the broadcast band. He leaned in, straining to parse the noise, but it dissolved into a flat, unnerving hum. A fresh hairline crack spiderwebbed from the corner of the small cabin window, tracing a faint, stark line across the fiberglass where none had been yesterday. It looked too neat, too deliberate, as if etched by something with an unnatural patience. He ran a hand over a yellowed photograph tacked to the wall – not Elias Thorne, but Elara Thorne, a smile frozen just before she vanished into the Blackwood Bay fog. The town was always whispering, but lately, the whispers felt less like rumors and more like encrypted messages. The air itself felt thin, stretched taut over something vast and unseen, and the integrity of everything—information, ground, sanity—felt like it was subtly, irrevocably unraveling.

He cast off the lines, the *Sea Ghost*'s small engine rumbling to life, adding its own vibration to the deeper hum beneath. As he idled past the old cannery, its rotting timbers groaning in the bruised, heavy air, his flashlight beam cut through the gloom. Faded graffiti, partially painted over, then resurfacing like a recurring nightmare, caught his eye: the sequence ‘7666’. The numbers weren’t a date, nor a tally. Just a stark, unsettling declaration on the peeling paint, too close to the ‘seven’ whispered on the radio, too close to the mysterious 7.76 coordinate.

The wind came then, not a steady coastal breath but a series of sudden, violent shoves, as if something immense was fighting against an unseen restraint above the churning grey of the inlet. He steered through the agitated current, feeling the low, insistent hum beneath his feet. He remembered the police report from the lodge, a minor incident at the old cannery—not theft, but a peculiar act of destruction. Someone had taken a fishing net, meticulously woven and hung to dry, and sliced it into precise, ragged strips, as if with a sudden, violent urge to dismantle. No motive, no suspects. Just the act itself, a senseless fracturing of order, mirroring old Man Hemlock’s sudden bellowing at the sky over a split fence post. Blackwood Bay had always hummed with its own peculiar rhythm, but lately, that hum felt like a fracture, like the world itself was pulling apart at the seams.

Soon, Blackwood Bay was just a muted smear of light behind him. The usual symphony of low static and distant gulls had quieted. Not silenced, not entirely, but *muted*. Replaced by a strange, almost conscious hush that felt less like peace and more like a held breath. The pier, now far behind, had thrummed with a faint, almost imperceptible vibration, a low frequency that resonated more in his teeth than his ears. Too subtle for a truck, too steady for a boat. It was a pressure, an unseen hand pressing down, humming against the very foundations of the world. A tremor in the deeper currents, not of water, but of something far more ancient and unsettling. This was Blackwood. The town had its own pulse, its own strange rhythms. But tonight, it felt like something vast and unseen had been on the verge of revealing itself, only to draw back at the last moment, leaving behind this peculiar silence and the ghost of a distant shockwave.

He kept a steady course, the fog clinging to the water, thicker now, churning against the hull with an agitated current. After an hour, a dark shape emerged from the mist – a rocky islet, crowned with the skeletal remains of an old lighthouse. David steered closer, cutting the engine, letting the *Sea Ghost* drift. He ran a gloved hand over the damp, rough-hewn stone of the old foundation, illuminated by his spotlight. The Blackwood Bay old-timers spoke of foundations that never truly settled, of the ground here having a memory, a refusal to let things lie buried. He focused on the faint scorch marks etched into the rock face, half-hidden by tenacious sea-moss. They weren’t from a clean fire, not truly. More like an attempt to cauterize something, an effort that had fallen short. Twice, perhaps. The air, or perhaps just his own anxiety, tasted of ozone. His gaze drifted to the murky water below, where the tide pulled at the wreckage of an old fishing skiff. It had been salvaged from the seabed just last week, yet already, a dark, viscous slime clung to its rotten planks, as if the depths were trying to reclaim it, or perhaps, keep it tethered to some ancient, stubborn secret. A futile struggle, he thought, against something that simply refused to die.

Back at the helm, David ran a finger along the faded margin of the old maritime chart, salvaged from the abandoned Blackwood Bay cannery office. The paper, brittle as dried leaves, showed depth soundings that no longer matched the shifting seabed. He was looking for discrepancies, for the places where the known world diverged from the official records, a habit born from years of chasing shadows. He felt a low, insistent hum beneath his feet, a vibration more felt than heard, like the distant churn of some immense, unseen engine. It wasn't the usual Blackwood Bay rumble of the fishing boats or the distant, complaining groan of the old drawbridge. This was deeper, more fundamental, subtly shifting the balance of the air in the room. He felt a fleeting disorientation, a sense that the compass in his gut had spun a full three-sixty. Outside, the familiar squall of gulls seemed to take on a strange, frantic edge, their cries veering wildly as if lost in a clear sky. He gripped the edge of the console, steadying himself. The tremor passed, or perhaps simply receded into the background static of the bay.

He leaned closer to the chart, his eye catching a penciled note, almost erased, marking a spot far beyond the usual shipping lanes. It was close to his coordinates. 7.76 nautical miles. He re-checked the GPS, aligning the boat. The hum intensified, no longer a background thrum but a resonating chord, vibrating through the hull, through the water, through his very bones. The temperature dropped sharply. The water around the *Sea Ghost* was unnaturally still, a dark mirror reflecting the too-sharp stars, yet beneath the surface, a subtle current, strong and deliberate, pulled at the boat. Then, he saw them. Not on the surface, but beneath. Pulsating lights, deep orange and a sickly green, blooming in the depths, casting eerie shadows that danced on the underside of his hull. The 'lights on the water' were here, but they were *under* the water, beckoning. And then came the sound. It wasn't a song in any human sense, but a low, guttural cadence, a chorus of frequencies that resonated with the omnipresent hum, a vibration that seemed to tear at the fabric of the air, distorting everything. The depth sounder on his console screamed, its display a jumble of impossible readings, indicating a colossal mass rising from the seabed. The 'song that pulled the tide' was real, a primordial bass note that threatened to unravel his sanity. As the lights pulsed brighter, a vast, dark shape began to resolve itself beneath the shimmering surface, immense and impossibly geometric, like a submerged, ancient gateway rising from the abyss. The water around it began to swirl, a silent, powerful vortex forming, pulling the *Sea Ghost* closer, inexorably, towards the heart of the enigma.
